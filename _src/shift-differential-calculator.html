What a shift differential is

A shift differential is extra pay for working less popular hours — evenings, nights, weekends or holidays. It's set by your employer or union contract, as a flat amount (such as $2 an hour for nights) or a percentage of your base rate (such as 10% for weekends). Federal law doesn't require one; it's common in healthcare, manufacturing, warehouses, retail and call centers.

Differential ($) = flat amount, or base rate × percent ÷ 100 Rate for those hours = base rate + differential Pay = base hours × base rate + differential hours × (base rate + differential)

Worked example: $18.00 base, $2.00 night differential, 16 night hours and 24 day hours

  1. Night rate: $18.00 + $2.00 = $20.00 an hour.
  2. Pay: 24 × $18.00 + 16 × $20.00 = $432.00 + $320.00 = $752.00.
  3. The differential added 16 × $2.00 = $32.00.

Shift differentials and overtime

Under federal rules, shift differentials are part of your regular rate — the rate overtime is based on. So when you work over 40 hours, overtime isn't just 1.5 × your base rate:

Worked example: 45 hours at $20.00, 15 of them on nights at +$2.00

  1. Straight-time pay: 45 × $20.00 + 15 × $2.00 = $930.00.
  2. Regular rate: $930.00 ÷ 45 = $20.67 an hour.
  3. Overtime premium: 5 hours × ½ × $20.67 = $51.67.
  4. Total: $981.67 — a little more than if overtime were based on $20.00 alone ($980.00).

Frequently asked questions

What is a typical night shift differential?

It varies a lot by industry and employer. Flat differentials of a dollar or a few dollars an hour, or percentages around 5% to 15%, are common; some healthcare jobs pay more for nights and weekends.

Is a shift differential required by law?

Not under federal law. It's paid when your employer's policy or your union contract says so.

Does the differential apply to the whole shift?

That depends on the policy: some pay it for the whole shift if most of it falls in the evening or night, others only for the hours inside the differential window.
